### Lesson 7 - The Time Lens for Businesses

**Thesis:** The same "time lens" you use on your own inbox/calendar/to-do list to spot repetitive, time-consuming, error-prone tasks also works on a business [00:00], but with one flip: with a business you don't already know their processes, so instead of reading their files you interview leadership and ask the right questions to surface the processes for them and for you [00:13], [00:44]. As the closing line puts it: "the difference is that you're now asking the questions instead of answering them" [04:54].

**Key teachings:**
- For yourself, your processes are "hiding in your routine" so you already know them; for a business you "don't know their processes yet or how they work" [00:14], [00:31].
- You are NOT going to look at each employee's calendar or emails, "instead, you're going to be interviewing leadership and asking the right questions" [00:31], [00:40].
- Your goal sitting with a CEO/founder/head of department is to "identify where manual labor is eating their time" [00:53], [01:04].
- Don't accept vague answers, "push for specific details… which team, what tasks, and how often… concrete details, not just generalizations" [01:35], [01:48].
- Reframe automation from "making things easier" to "unlocking valuable potential", a "way more interesting conversation for a manager" [02:23], [02:33].
- A task is a good automation candidate if it "happens more than once, follows a pattern and involves moving information" [03:26], [03:34].
- The mindset shift at the end: with a business "you're now asking the questions instead of answering them" [04:54].

**Frameworks / steps:**
- The two anchor questions to ask a CEO/head of management:
  1. "Which department is currently the most expensive in terms of manual work?", gets them thinking about where human hours go on repetitive low-value tasks [01:04], [01:19].
  2. "If we gave everyone on the team five hours back per week, what would they do with it?", does two things: quantifies the time being lost, and connects time savings to value [01:49], [02:17].
- The three areas where business tasks hide, each with probe questions [02:46], [03:22]:
  - **Communication** [02:48]: "are customer support agents answering the same questions over and over? Are salespeople sending the same follow-up emails? Are managers chasing the same status updates?"
  - **Data movement** [03:03]: "is anyone copying and pasting between systems? Are reports being manually pooled and formatted? Is data being entered in multiple places?"
  - **Processes** [03:13]: "what happens when a new lead comes in? What happens when a new employee is onboarded? What happens when a new order is placed?"
- The candidate filter: happens more than once + follows a pattern + involves moving information = probably automate it [03:26], [03:34].

**Scripts / templates:**
- The two interview questions (verbatim): "Which department is currently the most expensive in terms of manual work?" [01:07] · "If we gave everyone on the team five hours back per week, what would they do with it?" [01:49].

**Pitfalls:**
- Letting leadership answer vaguely, "don't let them answer vaguely… you actually want concrete details, not just generalizations" [01:35], [01:48].
- Trying to discover processes by snooping each employee's calendar/emails instead of interviewing leadership [00:31], [00:40].

### Lesson 8 - The Income Lens for Businesses

**Thesis:** The "time lens" finds where hours are lost; the income lens finds where growth is blocked [00:00]. These are not the same thing, and the income lens is the one that matters more because it lets you stop selling efficiency and start selling money [00:17], finding the one process (the constraint) that's blocking a business from growing past its current revenue level [00:27]. That is the language leadership actually cares about [02:36].

**Key teachings:**
- Time saved and revenue unlocked are different things, you can save 10 hours a week on a process that doesn't affect revenue at all, or automate one bottleneck and unlock a big jump in capacity [00:11].
- Every business has a constraint: the bottleneck that breaks first under more volume. Whatever breaks first IS the constraint, and that's where automation has the highest leverage [01:02].
- Reframe automation as revenue, not efficiency: faster lead response → more closed deals; more capacity → more clients without hiring; fewer errors → lower costs [01:55].
- When you connect automation to money, "let's do this because it's more efficient" becomes "let's do this because it unlocks the growth you're leaving on the table" [00:48], a fundamentally different and stronger sale.

**Frameworks / steps:**
- The two bottleneck-finding questions to ask leadership [00:38]:
  1. "If you double your leads tomorrow, what is going to break first?", forces them to name what's limiting their scale [00:41]. (Watch for answers like: "we couldn't respond fast enough, so leads would go cold" / "our onboarding team is overwhelmed" / "our sales team is already at capacity" [00:51].)
  2. "What process is currently [keeping] you [at] your current revenue level?", a different angle for leaders who think in terms of what's holding them back rather than what breaks [01:09]. (Watch for: "we can only take on X clients because of our process" / "our team spends so much time on another task we can't focus on sales" / "we're turning away work because we don't have capacity" [01:25].)
- Then connect the dots between automation and money for them, using the three revenue equations [01:55]: faster lead response = more closed deals; increased capacity = more clients; fewer errors = lower costs.

**Scripts / templates:**
- Closing pitch frame, near-verbatim [02:45]: "Time savings are great, but the real question is what's [capping] your growth? Most businesses have a bottleneck, a process that limits how much they can scale. Maybe it's how fast you can respond to leads. Maybe it's your onboarding capacity. Or maybe it's a manual step that breaks every time volume spikes. What I do is I'm going to find that bottleneck and I will show you exactly how to remove it. Not just to save time, but to unlock the growth you are currently [leaving] on the table." [02:45, 03:16]
- (Note: the transcript's auto-captioned words "copying"/"dethroning"/"living on the table" are transcription errors for "capping"/"keeping"/"leaving on the table", corrected above for sense.)

**Numbers / rules:**
- "A business can save 10 hours a week on a process that doesn't affect revenue at all", 10 hours/week, illustrative [00:11].
- "Automate one bottleneck and unlock a 30% increase in capacity", 30%, illustrative [00:16].
- No pricing, fees, or income figures are given anywhere in this lesson. The two numbers above are illustrative examples, not rules.
- Rule: frame automation in terms of revenue impact, not just time savings [02:31]. Rule: whatever breaks first is the constraint, and that's where automation has the highest leverage [01:02].

**Pitfalls:**
- Selling time savings alone, leadership doesn't care as much; you must translate it into revenue [02:31].
- Pitching efficiency ("let's do this because it's more efficient") instead of growth, a weaker conversation [01:49].
- Automating the wrong process, saving 10 hours on something that doesn't move revenue, instead of the one bottleneck that's actually capping growth [00:11].
- Forgetting that some leaders think in "what breaks" terms and others in "what's holding us back" terms, that's why there are two questions, not one [01:14].

**How King applies it:**
- King's bottleneck is selling/closing, not building, this lesson is his closing script. On any Upwork bid or warm chat, stop leading with "I'll save you time" and instead ask the discovery question first: "If you doubled your leads tomorrow, what would break first?" Let the prospect name their own constraint, then aim his demo at exactly that.
- His huge demo library + live AI demos become proof for whichever bottleneck surfaces: if they say "we can't respond to leads fast enough," show the relevant lead-response/AI demo; if "our team's maxed out," show an automation that adds capacity. One question picks the demo, instead of guessing.
- Use the near-verbatim closing frame [02:45] in proposals and the /upwork and /apply drafts: open with "what's capping your growth?", name a likely bottleneck (lead response / onboarding / a manual step that breaks under volume), then promise to find and remove it, "not just to save time, but to unlock the growth you're leaving on the table." This is honest and faith-aligned: it sells a real outcome, no hype.
- Translate every pitch into one of the three money equations [01:55], faster lead response = more closed deals, more capacity = more clients, fewer errors = lower costs, so a non-technical buyer instantly sees the euros, which is what moves a €0-earned freelancer to a first paying client.

### Lesson 9 - The SOP Audit: Inventory vs Creation

**Thesis:** You cannot automate a process that is not defined [00:33]. Before selling or building any automation, audit the business to see what's actually documented, what only lives in people's heads, and what gaps must be filled, and the audit itself (plus writing the missing SOPs) is a paid deliverable that comes *before* the automation [04:42, 04:59].

**Key teachings:**
- Most businesses think they have documented processes but don't: when you ask to see the SOPs, you get "a Google Doc from 2019 that no one's updated" or "Sarah knows how this works", tribal knowledge [00:10, 00:21].
- Trying to automate an undefined process is "like trying to build an app without the requirements document", you discover edge cases aren't covered and the outcome isn't what they expected [00:45, 00:51].
- Auditing first saves both you and the client a headache and reveals whether processes are well-defined enough to automate at all [00:53, 01:03].
- The audit has a double benefit: it organises the processes (better for you when automating) AND you can charge for the SOP creation, because it's valuable information for the client [03:22, 03:32].
- Pitching the audit "shows that you understand their business deeply, and it's going to create value before you even build any automation" [04:31, 04:36].

**Frameworks / steps:** Three steps to audit a company [01:03]:
1. **Ask what exists** [01:03], ask "can you show me the documentation for this process?" Don't take a yes for an answer; ask to see it and review it: Is it complete? Is it up to date? Does it cover edge cases? Most of the time it's outdated, incomplete, or barely scratches the surface [01:08, 01:26].
2. **Identify the gaps** [01:30], compare what's written to what actually happens. Ask "is this still how your team does it?", "what happens when X goes wrong?", "who handles the exceptions?" Expect a gap between what's on paper and what people actually do [01:30, 01:53].
3. **Find the tribal knowledge** [01:48], the rules that live in people's heads, undocumented (e.g. "we always CC the operations team on that," "if the amount is over 10k, it goes to Mike for approval"). These are the hidden rules; find them before automating [01:53, 02:16].

**Output of the audit** [02:16]: a clear list of every process, its documentation status, and its gap. Three worked examples given [02:27, 03:06]:
- *Lead follow-up*, documentation **partial** (a Notion doc, but outdated); gap = missing rules, no edge cases documented.
- *Client onboarding*, documentation **none** (Sarah handles it, not written anywhere); the entire process needs documenting.
- *Support ticket triage*, documentation **partial** (basic flow chart, doesn't cover some scenarios); gap = missing AI classification criteria.

**Scripts / templates:** Two near-verbatim scripts.

1. **Audit questions** to surface gaps + tribal knowledge [01:36, 02:11]:
   - "Is this still how your team does it?"
   - "What happens when X goes wrong?"
   - "Who handles the exceptions?"

2. **The client pitch for the SOP deliverable** [03:52, 04:25]:
   > "Before we can automate this process, we need to document exactly how it works, including all the exceptions and edge cases your team handles." (Show them the chart / audit results.) "Right now, this process lives in Sarah's head, if she leaves, it's going to live with her. This other process is incomplete, and this other process is missing documentation entirely. And I can help you identify that. I will interview your team. I will document the process step by step, and I will deliver a complete SOP. And once that is done, we will have a clear foundation to build the automation on."

**Numbers / rules:** No pricing or quantitative figures are given for the audit or SOP creation. The only numbers in the transcript are inside example tribal-knowledge rules, not instructions: "if the amount is over 10k, it goes to Mike for approval" [02:04, 02:11], and "Google Doc from 2019" as the image of stale documentation [00:10, 00:16]. Hard rule stated: "You cannot automate a process that is not defined" [00:33] / "I suggest you don't... you can't automate a process that is not defined" [04:42, 04:48].

**Pitfalls:**
- Taking "yes, we have SOPs" at face value, always ask to actually see and review the documentation [01:14, 01:19].
- Assuming written documentation matches reality, there's usually a gap between paper and practice [01:41, 01:53].
- Jumping straight into automation before finding tribal knowledge, the hidden rules will surface later as broken edge cases [02:11, 02:16].
- Waiting for the client to document the gaps themselves, "they probably won't"; offer to do it (and get paid) instead [03:46, 03:51].

**How King applies it:** This is the missing front-half of King's sales motion. His bottleneck is closing, not building, and this lesson gives him a paid first step that opens the door before any automation is promised. When a warm lead or Upwork prospect describes a process they want automated, King doesn't jump to a demo; he runs the 3-step audit conversation first ("can you show me the documentation?" → the three gap questions → surface the tribal knowledge), then hands them the process/status/gap table. That table is a small, low-risk, paid deliverable (an SOP-writing engagement) he can sell *before* committing to a full automation build, a fast route to his first paying client, EUR 0 → first invoice. It fits faith-first/honest selling perfectly: he's genuinely organising their business and being upfront that automation can't safely happen until the process is defined, rather than overselling a build. Practically, King should turn the 3-step audit + the process/status/gap table + the "Sarah's head" pitch script into a `/sop-audit` skill (WAT: workflow = the 3 steps, agent = Claude runs the discovery, tool = generates the gap table, skill = the command), and slot it into `/upwork` and `/apply` as the opening offer when a prospect's need is process-automation rather than a website/video.

### Lesson 10 - Scoring Before Mapping (Business ICE)

**Thesis:** When you sit with a client after auditing their processes, you don't tell them what to automate first, you score each process *together* using the same ICE framework (Impact, Confidence, Ease) you used on yourself in phase one. The scoring is really about *alignment*: getting leadership to own the decision and pick their own top one or two priorities, which becomes your first paid project [00:01, 00:11, 00:17].

**Key teachings:**
- It's the *same* ICE framework from phase one, but now run *with the client*, not alone, "you're going to do it together" [00:11].
- The real product of the session isn't just a ranked list, it's *alignment*: "getting everyone on the same page about what matters, what's realistic and where to start" [00:17, 00:25].
- Scoring is a *conversation, not a presentation*, you guide them through the framework so they reach the answer themselves [00:46-00:53, 01:43-02:02].
- Letting leadership score it themselves does two things: (1) they *own the decision*, "it's no longer your opinion, it's their priorities"; (2) it *builds trust* because you're aligned with what they truly need [02:02-02:22].
- You are NOT pushing the project that's more fun, more interesting, or more money for you, you're helping them find *their* highest-value opportunity [02:22-02:29].
- The scored list becomes the "opportunity map," which is the input for the live blueprint / wireframe built in the next video [02:34, 02:47].

**Frameworks / steps (with [mm:ss]):**
1. After the SOP audit, you have a list of processes; walk the client through ICE scoring [00:30].
2. First, *explain what ICE scoring is*, in case they're not familiar with it [00:38].
3. Then go through each process *one by one* [00:38].
4. Score **Impact** 1-10, e.g. "how much time will this save per week" [00:46].
5. Score **Confidence** 1-10, ask: "is the process clearly defined, are there any unknown edge cases, or is it similar to something that's been done before" [00:46-01:05].
6. Score **Ease** 1-10, ask: "how many systems are involved, how complex is the logic, how long will it take to build" [01:05-01:16].
7. End state: a scored list + alignment on the top one or two priorities = a clear first project [02:34].
8. This becomes the *opportunity map*, the input for the blueprint/wireframe in the next video [02:47].
- (Note: at [01:05] Nate says "set a score for ice from one to 10", by context this is the Ease step, a verbal slip, not a fourth letter.)

**Scripts / templates:** No formal word-for-word script given, but Nate provides the near-verbatim diagnostic questions to ask the client per dimension:
- Impact: "how much time will this save per week from one to 10" [00:46].
- Confidence: "is the process clearly defined, are there any unknown edge cases or is it similar to something that's been done before" [00:55].
- Ease: "how many systems are involved, how complex is the logic, how long will it take to build" [01:05].
- Posture line to remember: "scoring and prioritizing with the client is a conversation. It's not a presentation. You are not supposed to tell what to prioritize. You're going to be guiding them through a framework that is going to identify the answer to[gether]" [01:43-01:53].

**Numbers / rules (with [mm:ss]):**
- All three dimensions are scored on a **1 to 10** scale [00:46].
- **Ease:** "one means it's going to be very complicated... and 10 is going to be very easy, super simple to build" [01:16].
- **Confidence:** "one means I'm not confident at all that we will build this properly and 10 means I'm very confident that this is going to be built properly" [01:26].
- **Impact:** "one means it's not going to make any impact at all for the company and 10 means it's going to make a huge impact" [01:35].
- Target outcome = alignment on the **top one or two priorities** [02:34]. (No threshold score, no weighting formula, and no "automate above X" cutoff is given.)

**Pitfalls:**
- Treating it as a *presentation* where you tell the client what to prioritize, wrong; it must be a guided *conversation* [01:43-01:53].
- Pushing the project *you* prefer (more fun, more interesting, more money for you) instead of their highest-value one [02:22-02:29].
- Skipping the explanation, Nate says to first explain what ICE is in case they aren't familiar [00:38], so don't assume they know the framework.
- (Watch the [01:05] verbal slip, "score for ice" really means the Ease score; don't mistake it for a fourth dimension.)

**How King applies it:** King's bottleneck is *closing*, not building, and he's faith-first/honest, this lesson is tailor-made for that. When a warm lead or an Upwork client replies, King shouldn't open by pitching whichever flashy demo from his library he'd most enjoy building. Instead he runs a short discovery, lists their processes, then *scores them together* on Impact/Confidence/Ease (1-10 each), letting the client name their own top one or two. That does three honest things at once: it makes the client *own* the choice (so it's their priority, not his sell), it *builds trust* (the exact thing that converts a €0 freelancer into a first paid client), and it points him to the project he can deliver with high confidence, protecting his reputation on job number one. Practically, he can fold this into `/upwork` and `/apply` as a "discovery + ICE-score" step before he picks which demo to attach, and reach for the high-Confidence, high-Impact item first so the first deliverable actually lands. It also aligns perfectly with his existing Rule #13 (discovery-first), ICE scoring *is* the structured discovery, and the faith-honest framing ("help them find their highest-value opportunity, not the one that pays me most" [02:22-02:29]) is exactly King's "do it God's way."

### Lesson 11 - The Live Blueprint: Mapping in the Room

**Thesis:** After you've audited a company and picked the highest-ROI process, don't diagram it alone at your desk. Draw the process live, in front of the CEO, on a shared canvas. Watching their mess become a clear picture (and seeing exactly where it breaks and how you'd fix it) gives you "visual authority" and triggers the "aha moment" where they decide it's worth paying for [00:16]-[00:48].

**Key teachings:**
- Wireframing for a client is the same skill you already learned diagramming for yourself in Phase 2, just done in front of the CEO instead of alone [00:18].
- "Visual authority" = the moment the CEO's messy mental picture becomes clear on screen; you stop being "someone who knows about automation" and become "the person who gets their business" [00:22]-[00:31].
- The "aha moment" is when they realise this is worth paying for [00:40]-[00:48].
- When a client sees their own problem on a diagram, they see that you understand the problem AND exactly how you'll fix it, and "in that specific moment in general is when they buy" [00:65 area / 01:09]... corrected: [01:09] "in that specific moment in general is when they buy because you're showing them value."
- You walk them through step by step, drawing as you go, then point to the bottleneck and quantify the pain [02:54]-[03:03].
- Practising out loud beforehand makes you smoother, and the smoother you are, the more confidence you project [04:58]-[05:05].

**Frameworks / steps:**

The 5-step live wireframe (draw each node as you narrate) [01:46]-[02:50]:
1. **Trigger**, start with what kicks it off. Draw the trigger node [01:54].
2. **Data flow**, where the data goes; draw the data source and destination [02:04].
3. **Decision points**, the logic / conditions; draw the conditional branches [02:13].
4. **AI steps**, where AI replaces a human decision (e.g. classifying); draw the AI node and explain what it does and why [02:26].
5. **Output**, the final result (e.g. a notification); draw the output node [02:40].

The transition into wireframing, then the loop [01:39]-[02:54]:
- After the opportunity map is done and the top process is picked, transition in.
- Open the canvas, walk the CEO through step by step, drawing as you go.
- Point to the bottleneck and quantify the time it costs.

The 10-minute practice exercise [03:51]-[04:58]:
1. Pick a process (yours or a client's, it doesn't matter, you're practising presentation) [04:03].
2. Set a timer for 10 minutes [04:16].
3. Open a canvas (Excalidraw, Canva, Mural, or pen and paper) [04:29].
4. Explain the process out loud as if presenting, step by step, logic/conditions, where AI helps and why, the outputs, drawing the wireframe in real time as you explain [04:33].
5. Repeat with different processes until it feels natural [04:52].

### Lesson 12 - Should You Charge for the Opportunity Map?

**Thesis:** Whether you charge for an Opportunity Map (a paid strategy document that finds where AI automation saves a business time/money, no code built yet) depends entirely on the trust you've earned. With no credibility, charging upfront is "one of the fastest ways to get rejected" [04:46]; as a beginner you either do a free/pay-after map to *earn* trust, or skip the map entirely and prove yourself with one small automation first.

**Key teachings:**
- "Nobody pays for advice from someone they don't trust." [00:27] Trust comes from either a relationship or a reputation, "if you have neither, you have a problem." [00:54]
- Credibility is built three ways: published content, client results, and industry experience [01:04]. "Don't worry about having a million followers, personally I started from zero." [01:24] Proof beats pitch: a story of saving a business 15 hours/week "is going to do so much more work than any pitch that you'll ever build." [01:48]
- The Opportunity Map is a *strategy document*, not the build, "no code is written in this step yet." [00:49]
- The map is "the beginning of business opportunities... You are now the person who understands their business best" [02:34], every opportunity on it is a future automation you're positioned to win.
- Even a free map needs baseline trust; "even a free opportunity map may be tough to sell" to a complete stranger [06:19], be strategic about who you approach first.
- Start with your network, people who already know and trust you, not cold strangers [06:32].

**Frameworks / steps:**
- Three-scenario decision framework [13:23, 12:19]:
  1. **Charge upfront**, only if you already have authority: content showing capability + results/portfolio/referrals [13:25].
  2. **Pay-after model**, beginner *with* a strong professional network: deliver value first, ask to be paid only if they find it useful [11:48].
  3. **Skip the map**, beginner with no authority *and* no network: build one small automation first to prove value, then the map becomes a natural next step [12:04].
- Two versions of the map [07:53]:
  - **Light version** [08:07], a one-hour call on their biggest pain points + a simple diagram of top opportunities; "a couple hours including the follow-up document" [08:25]; the one you "might do for free" [08:30].
  - **Deep dive** [08:46], interview department heads, review SOPs, map workflows org-wide; "may take days or even weeks" [08:57]; produces a strategy document with prioritized recommendations, estimated ROIs, and an implementation roadmap [09:08]; "the version that you always want to charge for, always, even as a beginner using the pay-after model" [09:09].
  - Use the light version to lead into the (paid) deep dive [09:34].
- Delivery / closing tips [12:42, 13:47]:
  1. After delivering, schedule a follow-up to present it live, "don't just send it over email" [12:47], to build authority and trust.
  2. Lead with quick wins, put the fastest, highest-impact, low-risk automations first [13:08]; once the first project goes well, the next ones sell easier [13:24].
  3. Package the build two ways: per-individual-project (good for small/cautious clients) or discounted bundle packages (good for larger/fast-moving clients) [13:47, 14:23].

**Scripts / templates:**
- Pay-after pitch [04:55]: *"I'm going to review your process and identify where AI can save you time and money. You're not going to have any upfront costs. And if you find it valuable, I would ask for a thousand or two thousand. And if you don't find it useful, you owe me nothing."*
- Warm-network opener [06:33]: *"Hey, I'm deep into AI for the past six months. And I've realized there are huge opportunities in businesses like yours. I would love to go through your processes and show you where automation could help. If you find it valuable, I would ask for a thousand or two thousand for the work. But if not, you owe me nothing."*
- Light-map → deep-dive bridge [09:42]: *"These are the ones that I could identify from our conversation, but if I spent time with each of your department heads and went through your actual SOPs, I'm confident we could find so much more."*
- Small-automation → map upsell [10:50]: *"This lead follow-up system is saving you five hours a week. I'm confident that there are other areas in your business where we could find similar or bigger wins. Would you like me to do a full process review of it?"*

**Numbers / rules:**
- Some consultants charge $5,000, $10,000, even $50,000 for an Opportunity Map, "for the strategy document," not the build [02:19]. These are non-beginners with track records, case studies, testimonials, serving companies with "more than hundreds of employees" [03:00].
- Pay-after ask: "a thousand or two thousand" [04:55] (also [06:33], [07:06]).
- Example proof point: saving a business "15 hours a week" [01:48]; lead follow-up saving "five hours a week" [10:55].
- Light version time: "a couple hours, including the follow-up document" [08:25]; deep dive: "days or even weeks" [08:57].
- Rule: "Charging upfront with no credibility is one of the fastest ways to get rejected. So I recommend that you don't do this yet." [04:46]
- Rule: "The deep dive is going to be the version that you always want to charge for, always, even as a beginner using the pay-after model." [09:09]
- Rule: "The opportunity map is not the last step, it's not the final goal, it is actually the beginning for future business engagements." [02:73→13:00 / 02:31].

**Pitfalls:**
- Charging upfront before you have any credibility, kills the deal instantly [04:36], [04:46].
- Pitching a full Opportunity Map to a cold business with no relationship, reputation, or referrals, "too much too soon," asking for huge access/trust before proving anything [09:58].
- Cold-pitching strangers instead of starting with your warm network, even a free map is hard to sell to someone who's never heard of you [06:14].
- Sending the finished map over email instead of presenting it live, you lose the authority/trust moment that leads to the build [12:47].
