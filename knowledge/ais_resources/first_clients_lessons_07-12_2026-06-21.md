### Lesson 7 - Creating Your Outreach List

**Thesis:** Everyone already has a warm list, you don't need new leads, you need to recognize and organize the contacts you already have, because almost everyone's network is far larger than they realize but it's "invisible" (scattered across phone, email, and social) [00:00], [00:47]. The single task of this lesson is to make that network visible by surfacing the people who already recognize your name [01:04], [03:35].

**Key teachings:**
- The beginner's first thought is "I don't know anyone," so they assume they must find leads (scraping LinkedIn, buying cold email lists, running ads, reaching out to strangers), but this instinct is usually wrong [00:10], [00:28].
- The fastest place to begin is NOT strangers; it's people who already recognize your name [00:28], [00:33].
- The problem isn't that your network doesn't exist, the problem is that it's invisible because your contacts are scattered everywhere [00:45], [01:02].
- Once names are written down in one place, the business "starts to feel real" and outreach becomes executable instead of hypothetical [01:04], [01:17].
- You don't need a brand, website, polished offer, team, or big audience before you reach out, that belief just creates delay. You need "one person, one painful problem, and one conversation" [01:17], [01:43].
- "Warm" means recognition, not friendship, warm doesn't mean best friend or that you talk regularly; it just means they recognize your name [00:67? → 02:08], [02:18].
- Reaching out to strangers means building attention and trust at the same time (hard); reaching a warm contact removes most of that friction, they're more likely to respond and it feels less intimidating [02:18], [02:38].
- Separate LIST-BUILDING from OUTREACH, in this lesson you are NOT sending messages, explaining your offer, or booking calls; your only job is to identify possible business owners [03:42], [03:54].
- Do tasks sequentially, not in parallel, "You'll win faster if you do things sequentially rather than trying to run them in parallel" [04:11], [04:15].
- Volume over perfection: "Volume matters way more than perfection at this stage", if someone might be connected to a business, add them; you can refine later [03:31? → 03:36], [04:35? → confirmed at 03:31/04:35 region].

**Frameworks / steps:**
- **The 3 places warm contacts live** [02:51], [03:26]:
  1. Your email inbox and contact history [02:55].
  2. Your social media connections, followers, people you follow, and old DM conversations [03:06].
  3. Your phone contacts ("one of the most overlooked lead lists that exists") [03:16].
- **The one-question filter (against overfiltering)** [04:34]: "Would this person recognize my name if they saw a message from me? If the answer is yes, throw them all in that list." [04:35], [04:41].
- **The build-the-list procedure** [04:41], [05:32]:
  1. Open a basic spreadsheet, no CRM needed [04:46], [04:51].
  2. Create five columns: **Name · Business or industry · Platform (where you know them from) · Last interaction (if you remember it) · Notes** [04:54], [05:01].
  3. Move through your platforms one at a time: Gmail contacts/inbox → Instagram followers and DMs → LinkedIn connections (if you use it) → phone contacts [05:09], [05:20].
  4. As you go, ask the screening question: "Does this person own, operate or influence a business?" If "might be yes," add them, don't overthink it [05:20], [05:30].
- **Action step / recap** [05:53], [06:13]: Build a list of your first 100 names, open a blank spreadsheet, make the five columns, search inbox + followers + DMs + phone contacts until you have 100 names. If you have enough contacts, add 100 per day until you reach 1,000.

**Scripts / templates:**
- Spreadsheet template (verbatim columns) [04:54], [05:01]: "Name, business or industry. Platform where you know them from. Last interaction if you remember it. And notes."
- The filter question (verbatim) [04:35], [04:38]: "Would this person recognize my name if they saw a message from me?"

**Numbers / rules:**
- 5 columns in the spreadsheet: Name / Business or industry / Platform / Last interaction / Notes [04:54], [05:01].

### Lesson 8 - What to Say in Your DMs

**Thesis:** Direct outreach beats indirect small talk: be clear from the first message about what you're doing and ask if they know someone who might benefit, because clarity is honest, low-pressure, and easy to respond to [00:00], [01:31].

**Key teachings:**
- Many people overcomplicate outreach by easing in with small talk (complimenting a post, vague questions about someone's business, chatting about something unrelated) [00:12], [00:23].
- The intention behind small talk is good (being polite, avoiding sounding transactional), but in practice it creates a conversation that never moves anywhere, small talk begets small talk and gets stuck in limbo, and then transitioning to the real reason feels weird [00:25], [00:50].
- Direct outreach does three things: (1) people immediately understand why you're reaching out, (2) it's easier for them to help/connect you right away, (3) you can reach out to more people without overthinking every message [00:53], [01:10].
- Your goal is to share what you're working on and ask a simple question, not to spark a long conversation [01:10], [01:14].
- Most people don't mind direct communication; what they dislike is pressure. As long as the message is honest and low-pressure, being direct is appreciated because it respects their time and removes confusion [01:15], [01:31].
- Keep messages simple, when messages get long, people stop reading and it starts sounding like marketing instead of a normal conversation [04:46-line]; "Simple scales, fancy fails" [03:59].
- Outreach is a numbers game / probability: some reply quickly, some later, some never; what matters is continuing to send and start conversations, and it gets easier with each message [04:21], [04:35].
- Volume matters more than perfection (the same rule as building the list in the previous lesson) [04:13], [04:17].

**Frameworks / steps:**
Nate's **four-part outreach framework** [01:33], [01:36]:
1. **Greeting + context**, normal greeting, then if relevant mention how you know them / where you're connected [01:39], [01:44]. (e.g. "hey Sarah, we met at the marketing meetup last year" [01:45]). Purpose: reminds them who you are + makes it feel natural, not random [01:54], [01:59].
2. **What you're working on**, briefly explain what you're working on; gives context without sounding like a sales pitch; a quick snapshot, no technical details, no full service explanation [02:02], [02:22].
3. **The simple ask**, the most important part: ask whether they know someone who might benefit; it works because it's easy to answer, you're not asking them to buy or putting them on the spot, and people like helping their network [02:22], [02:41].
4. **Remove all pressure (case study offer)**, say you're not trying to sell anything right now; you'd do the first one for free in exchange for a case study; this reframes it from a sales pitch to a free experiment, which makes introductions easier [02:41], [03:09].

**Action step** [04:35], [05:02]: open the contact list from the previous lesson → choose **5 people** from your network → send each one a message using the four-part framework → then go back to the spreadsheet and mark those contacts as "contacted." Checkpoint = **5 messages sent.**

**Scripts / templates:**
- Opener/context examples [01:45], [01:53]: "hey Sarah, we met at the marketing meetup last year." / "Hey Mike, saw your post about scaling your agency." / "Hey Alex, we're connected through the freelancer group."
- What-you're-working-on example [02:09], [02:14]: "I've been working on AI automation projects recently and helping businesses streamline parts of their operations."
- The ask example [02:26], [02:30]: "do you know anyone who might be interested in automating something in their business?"
- Remove-pressure example [02:49], [02:54]: "I'm not trying to sell anything right now. I'd actually do the first one for free in exchange for a case study."
- **Full combined message** [03:12], [03:29]: "Hey John, we met at the marketing meetup last year. I've been working on AI Automation Products recently and helping businesses streamline parts of their operations. Do you know anyone who might be interested in automating something in their business? I'm not trying to sell anything right now. I'd actually do the first one for free in exchange for a case study."

**Numbers / rules:**
- **Four-part** outreach framework [01:33].
- Three things direct outreach achieves [00:53].
- Offer to **do the first one for free** in exchange for a case study [02:51] / [03:00-03:01].
- Action step: choose / send to **5 people**; checkpoint = **5 messages sent** [04:40], [05:02].
- No price, no other ratio, no timeframe, and no reply-rate number is given, "no specific number given" for those.

**Pitfalls:**
- Easing in with small talk / vague questions / unrelated chat, creates a conversation stuck in limbo and makes the real ask feel weird later [00:15], [00:50].
- Applying pressure, people don't mind directness, they dislike pressure [01:19], [01:21].
- Beginners "improving" the message by adding more explanation and technical details to sound impressive, this makes it worse; long messages get unread and sound like marketing [03:36], [03:55].
- Overthinking / over-editing, spending a week (or too long) editing and second-guessing instead of sending; "Don't spend a week editing your message. Just write it and send it five times." [04:00], [04:08], [04:49], [04:55].
- Sending one or two messages then immediately analyzing results, outreach is a probability game; keep sending [04:17], [04:32].

**How King applies it:**
- This lesson is **warm-network outreach**, the channel King has NOT been working, he's been deep in Upwork (cold). It's the fastest honest path to his **first paying client** because it leverages people who already know him. Make a contact list (the previous lesson's step) of people he actually knows: Amazon DCEO colleagues, parish friends (John, Olly), Fr Bogdan's circle, gym/old contacts, family.
- Pick **5 people** and send the four-part message exactly as Nate teaches, adapted to King's real arsenal and voice. Example in his words: "Hey [name], it's King, [how you know me]. I've been building AI tools and websites for businesses lately, helping them save time and look premium online. Do you know anyone who might want a website or some automation in their business? I'm not trying to sell anything, I'd actually do the first one free in exchange for a case study." This is **100% honest and low-pressure**, which fits his faith-first, no-deception rule perfectly.
- The "free first one in exchange for a case study" is the key unlock for €0-earned King: it removes the price objection, gives him his first real testimonial/portfolio proof, and matches his existing **demo-first / free-sample** instinct, but here the deliverable is a real finished project, not just a demo.
- Keep it **short and human**, King's natural plain-English voice already fits "simple scales, fancy fails." Do NOT pile in his 9 services, 3D/immersive jargon, or technical detail; one snapshot sentence only. Run anything before sending through `/humanize` so it sounds like him, not AI.
- **Volume over perfection:** don't agonise. Send 5 today, mark them "contacted" in a simple list, and treat it as probability, some won't reply, that's expected. This complements (does not replace) the Upwork A+B loop; warm DMs may close his first deal faster than cold bids.
- Faith/honesty note: this method is fully aligned with King's values, it's clear, respects the other person's time, applies zero pressure, and offers genuine free value first.

### Lesson 9 - How to Get People on a Discovery Call

**Thesis:** When someone responds positively to your outreach, your only goal is to move them to a short call, not to explain everything, pitch, send a proposal, or solve the problem in the messages [00:00]. A call lets you understand their business far faster than messaging ever could [00:39].

**Key teachings:**
- When someone shows interest it feels like the moment to prove yourself, but none of the "racing brain" reactions (explain everything, describe automation in detail, send a proposal, pitch right away) should happen [00:09], [00:25].
- DMs and emails are great for *opening* conversations because they let you reach people quickly, but messaging is slow, tone is easily misunderstood, and real discussion is harder [00:29], [00:39].
- A call lets you understand how their business currently operates, ask about their processes, hear how they describe a problem, and see if there's a real opportunity to help [00:44], [00:56].
- For now (this lesson) the prime objective is just getting the conversation scheduled, the actual discovery call is taught in a later module [00:56], [01:05].
- The most common beginner mistake is trying to solve everything inside the messages, which turns into a long messy thread and confuses the business owner [01:05], [01:30].
- You do NOT need to understand the entire situation before asking for a call; trying to often slows everything down [01:30], [01:39]. Messaging isn't the right environment for diagnosing operational problems [01:39].
- HOW you invite matters: formal or pushy invitations make people hesitate; relaxed, helpful, conversational invitations get far more yeses [02:00], [02:13].
- This is consistent with the previous lesson's positioning: "Don't sell a service, offer insight", and the call is the easiest way to explore that insight [02:37], [02:41].
- Calls build trust faster than messaging because people hear your voice; "Messaging opens the door, but the call is where the relationship actually begins" [03:04], [03:18].
- Not everyone will want a call; a "not right now / we're good / maybe later" is usually about timing, not failure [03:18], [03:42].
- Business development = planting seeds: some opportunities appear immediately, others pop up months later when their workload grows, a process breaks, or they get more aware of automation [03:42], [03:56].
- Keep every interaction positive even on a "no", outreach is still part of the client experience; owners remember who replied politely vs who left them on read, and a polite no can lead to a referral later [03:56], [04:53].

**Frameworks / steps:**
- The core rule: when someone shows interest, "you only need to do one thing, offer a call" [01:52], [01:56].
- Next action step, "Booking your first business conversation" [04:53]:
  1. Go back to the outreach messages you sent in lesson 3.2 [04:58].
  2. Review the responses [05:03].
  3. If anyone replied positively or showed interest, send a simple call invitation [05:03].
  4. Keep the message simple, don't over-explain, just invite them to a short conversation [05:13], [05:17].
  5. Once the call is scheduled, update your tracking sheet and mark that contact as "conversation booked" [05:17], [05:25].
- Next lesson teaser: "the rule of a hundred," a framework from Alex Hormozi ("Alex Ramose" in the transcript) for outreach [05:25], [05:29].

**Scripts / templates (near-verbatim):**
- Soft offer of a call: "happy to take a look at your setup if you want. We can do a quick call." [01:56]
- Three relaxed invitation examples [02:13], [02:25]:
  1. "if you want, we can jump on a quick call and I can take a look at how you're handling it now."
  2. "happy to walk through it with you if that helps. A quick call is usually the easiest."
  3. "if you're open to it, we can do a short call and I can show you what I'm seeing."
- Polite "not right now" response: "no worries at all. If it ever becomes useful, feel free to reach out." [04:21], [04:26]
- Action-step invitation examples [05:03], [05:17]: "happy to take a look if you want. Let's jump on a quick call." / "if it helps, we could jump on a short call and walk through it."

**Numbers / rules:**
- "In 10 minutes of conversation, you can often understand more about a business than you could through 20 messages" [02:41], [02:46].
- The "20 responses / 20 messages" point, a thread can balloon to ~20 responses and become messy [01:21]; and a call beats 20 messages [02:41]. (These are illustrative figures Nate uses, not prices.)
- No prices, ratios, or timeframes given. References lesson "3.2" for the original outreach messages [04:58] and teases "the rule of a hundred" (Hormozi) for the next lesson [05:25], no specific number is explained for the rule of a hundred in this lesson.

**Pitfalls:**
- Trying to prove yourself / explain everything / pitch / send a proposal the moment someone shows interest [00:09], [00:25].
- Trying to solve the whole problem inside the messages → a 20-response, messy thread that confuses the owner [01:05], [01:30].
- Feeling you must understand the entire situation before asking for a call, it's unnecessary and slows things down [01:30], [01:39].
- Inviting in a formal or pushy way → people hesitate [02:00].
- Treating every "not right now" as rejection / a personal failure [03:18], [04:05].
- Leaving an uninterested person "on red" instead of responding politely, it costs you future business and referrals [04:17], [04:21].

When a prospect replies to a /apply or cold/warm outreach message, or an Upwork buyer responds, King's single next move is to offer a short call, NOT to dump his 9 services or pile on demos. One line, relaxed: e.g. "Happy to take a quick look at how you're handling it now, we can jump on a short call." [02:13]

### Lesson 10 - Use Mini Audits to Build Trust

**Thesis:** A free mini audit removes pressure and positions you as helpful instead of transactional [00:00], instead of selling automation, you offer "a second set of eyes" on one small process, which removes the business owner's two biggest fears (risk and uncertainty) and opens the door to real work later.

**Key teachings:**
- When people talk about their services they flip into "sales mode", explaining everything they do, justifying their value, rambling to prove they're the expert, and the conversation becomes about convincing someone to buy, which creates pressure on both sides [00:04, 00:33].
- The first mindset shift: "Stop trying to sell, start trying to actually help." [00:37] You're not asking them to buy; you're asking "would you like a second set of eyes on this process?" [00:50].
- Most business owners already understand the value of a second opinion, an outside perspective reveals things insiders don't notice, so a mini audit positions you as someone offering insight, not pushing a service [00:23, 01:12].
- The mini audit removes the two biggest concerns owners have: (1) **risk**, no payment, no commitment, no contracts/upfront costs/long implementation [01:22, 01:49]; (2) **uncertainty**, they don't need to understand automation yet, they just explain how something works today and you look for improvements [01:50, 02:15]. The conversation becomes about clarity, not commitment [02:11, 02:20].
- It's one of the best entry points early in your career, you learn real business operations while providing value [02:20, 02:29].
- Lead with the **outcome/result, not the technology**, business owners care about saving time, reducing repetitive work, fixing bottlenecks, smoother operations; tech is only valuable if it improves those outcomes [02:43, 03:03]. Leading with technical language too early makes the conversation stall [03:42, 03:52].
- Keep the **scope small**, don't turn the audit into a full process redesign or mapping entire operations; offer a quick look at how one thing currently works [03:52, 04:11]. The audit answers a single question: "Is there a better way for this process to work?" [04:24, 04:30]. Implementation comes later [04:30, 04:39].
- Use transparency over inflated credentials, you don't need to sound like a big agency or exaggerate experience; honesty ("I've been working on automation projects recently and building a few case studies") often builds more trust [05:29, 06:03].

**Frameworks / steps:**
- **The mindset shift:** Stop trying to sell → start trying to actually help (offer "a second set of eyes on this process") [00:37, 00:55].
- **The two concerns a mini audit removes:** 1) Risk (no payment/commitment) [01:22, 01:49]; 2) Uncertainty (they don't need to understand automation, just explain how something works today) [01:50, 02:15].
- **Lead with outcome, not tech**, reframe "I build automation systems" into a result-led statement [02:43, 03:14].
- **Keep the scope small**, the audit answers ONE question: "Is there a better way for this process to work?" [04:24, 04:30].
- **Audit positioning statement**, 1 to 3 short sentences that explain what you're offering; your "anchor" during outreach conversations [04:44, 04:53]. It does three things: (1) explains the outcome clearly, (2) removes pressure (it's free if small), (3) explains WHY you're offering it (building case studies) [05:03, 05:20].
- **Your action step:** write your positioning statement using the given structure (or your own version), then practice saying it out loud until it sounds natural and conversational [06:08, 06:29].

**Scripts / templates:**
- Reframe from tech to outcome [02:48, 03:14]:
  - "I build systems that automate the busy work inside businesses so your employees spend less time on manual tasks." [03:09]
  - "I help businesses automate repetitive processes so their teams can focus on higher value work." [03:14]
  - "I create systems that automate routine task[s] for employees so businesses can delay hiring additional staff and save money." [03:23]
- Audit positioning statement (the recommended template) [04:54, 05:03, repeated 06:08, 06:19]:
  - "I would love to chat with you about how your business works and see if I could build you something that saves money. Free of charge if it's just a small starter project. I'm just looking to build up some case studies." [04:54, 05:03]
- Honest credentials line [05:50, 05:54]:

- Examples of small audit "slices" you can offer to look at [04:11, 04:24]: how a company handles onboarding new clients; mapping their lead follow-up process; how internal reports are created each week; where manual data entry is happening.

**Numbers / rules:**
