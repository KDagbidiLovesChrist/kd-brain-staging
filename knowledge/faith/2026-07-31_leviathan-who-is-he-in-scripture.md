# Leviathan · who (and what) he is everywhere the Bible mentions him · ① The Word

**Date:** 2026-07-31  ·  **Track:** 📖 The Word (Bible study / Q&A)  ·  **Born from:** Adèola's question, studying with King

## 📖 In plain words (the easy version)
**Leviathan** (Hebrew *Liwyatan*, "the twisting / coiled one") is the Bible's great **sea monster**, a huge,
untameable creature of the deep. The Bible speaks of him in **three ways at once**, and all three are true:

1. **A creature God made.** However enormous he is, he is not a god and not God's rival. Psalm 104 says God
   made him **"to play in the sea"**, to God, the most terrifying thing in the ocean is like a goldfish in a
   bowl.
2. **A picture of chaos and untameable power.** In Job 41 God spends a whole chapter asking Job: *can YOU put
   a hook in Leviathan? Can you make him your pet?* Of course not. The point: if you cannot handle one of My
   creatures, trust Me with the things you cannot understand.
3. **A symbol of the enemy, the devil, whom God will destroy.** Isaiah 27:1 promises that "in that day" God
   will **slay** "Leviathan the fleeing serpent... the dragon in the sea." The Church Fathers read this as
   satan, and Revelation picks up the exact same picture: "the **dragon**, that **serpent of old**, who is
   the Devil and Satan" (Rev 20:2).

So the one-sentence answer: **Leviathan is the great sea creature the Bible uses as the living picture of
chaos and of the evil one, and every single mention exists to show that God alone is his Master.** The sea
monster the pagans feared as a god, Scripture demotes to a creature on God's leash.

## In one line
Leviathan is the untameable sea-dragon of Scripture, a creature to God, a terror to us, a figure of the
devil, and already defeated in Christ.

## ✨ Carry this on your heart
**"What is a monster to me is a plaything to my God."**
*Little prayer: "Lord, You who tamed the dragon, calm every chaos in my life."*

## Every place the Bible names him (the word "Leviathan" appears 6 times, in 5 passages)

### 1 · Job 3:8 — the curse-word of despair
> "May those curse it who curse the day, those who are ready to arouse Leviathan." (Job 3:8, NKJV)

Job, in his agony, curses the day of his birth and reaches for the darkest image his world knew: professional
cursers who would "wake the sea monster" to swallow a day whole. It shows Leviathan was already a byword for
**uncontrollable, devouring darkness**. Job is saying: let my birthday be swallowed by chaos.

### 2 · Job 41 — the whole chapter, God's own description (the longest passage)
> "Can you draw out Leviathan with a hook, or snare his tongue with a line which you lower?" (Job 41:1)

At the climax of the book, God answers Job from the whirlwind, and His final exhibit is Leviathan: 34 verses
of him. No hook can hold him, no harpoon pierces him, swords bounce off, he sneezes flashes of light, smoke
pours from his nostrils, "he laughs at the threat of javelins," and the chapter ends:

> "He beholds every high thing; **he is king over all the children of pride**." (Job 41:34)

**The point of the chapter:** God is not showing off an animal, He is asking Job, *if you cannot even
approach this creature of Mine, will you summon Me, its Maker, to court?* Job gets it, and repents (Job
42:1–6). Note: many scholars think the "natural model" behind the poetry is the **crocodile** (as Behemoth in
Job 40 may be the hippopotamus), but the language is deliberately bigger than any zoo animal, this is chaos
itself wearing scales. In the **Septuagint** (the Greek Old Testament the Orthodox Church uses), the word
here is **drakōn**, "the dragon."

### 3 · Psalm 74:13–14 (LXX Psalm 73) — the past victory
> "You divided the sea by Your strength; You broke the heads of the sea serpents in the waters. **You broke
> the heads of Leviathan in pieces**, and gave him as food to the people inhabiting the wilderness." (Ps 74:13–14)

Here Leviathan has **many heads**, and God has already crushed them. The psalmist is remembering the
**Exodus**: God split the Red Sea and shattered Pharaoh's power (the "sea monster" of Egypt), then fed His
people in the wilderness. It is a psalm sung in a national disaster, and its logic is: *You beat the dragon
before, You can do it again.*

### 4 · Psalm 104:26 (LXX Psalm 103) — the goldfish verse ⭐
> "There the ships sail about; there is that Leviathan which You have made **to play there**." (Ps 104:26)

The creation psalm. In the huge sea, alongside the ships, God points out Leviathan, made **to play** (the
Hebrew can even be read "whom You made **to play with**", God's plaything). The LXX says: "this **dragon**
whom You formed **to sport in it**."

**⛪ The Orthodox connection King will love:** Psalm 103 (LXX) is the **opening psalm of every Vespers**. So
every single evening, the Church begins her prayers by, among all creation, smiling at the sea-dragon God
made to play. The terror of the pagans is, in Orthodox worship, a daily proof of how big our God is.

### 5 · Isaiah 27:1 — the future victory (the prophecy)
> "In that day the LORD with His severe sword, great and strong, will punish **Leviathan the fleeing
> serpent, Leviathan that twisted serpent**; and He will slay the reptile [dragon] that is in the sea." (Isa 27:1)

Job = the present (God restrains him). Psalm 74 = the past (God crushed him at the Exodus). Isaiah = the
**future**: "in that day", the Day of the Lord, God will finally **kill** the serpent. This is no longer
about any animal; it is God's promise to destroy **evil itself**.

## 🖋️ The "God the Author" craft note (why the wording matters)
- **Isaiah quotes the pagans' own poetry to dethrone their god.** Centuries before Isaiah, the Canaanites at
  Ugarit sang of **Lotan/Litanu, "the fleeing serpent... the twisting serpent"**, a seven-headed sea monster
  their god Baal battled. Isaiah 27:1 uses the **same two epithets almost word-for-word**, and Psalm 74's
  "heads" (plural) matches the seven-headed monster. This is deliberate, inspired craft: Scripture takes the
  most famous monster-story of the neighbours and says, *your "epic battle" is no battle at all; the LORD
  simply made that thing, plays with it, and will slay it when He chooses.* The Bible does not copy the myth,
  it **demolishes** it (same move as Genesis 1:21, where the "great sea creatures", *tanninim*, that pagans
  worshipped are just item four on God's creation list).
- **The name itself:** *Liwyatan* comes from a root meaning "to twist / coil", the "wreathed one." The LXX's
  choice of **drakōn** is the thread that lets the New Testament pick the image up.
- **Leviathan's relatives in Scripture** (same image, other names): **Rahab** the sea monster (Job 26:12–13,
  "His hand pierced the fleeing serpent"; Ps 89:9–10; Isa 51:9–10, where slaying Rahab/the dragon = the Red
  Sea crossing again), **Behemoth** his land-twin (Job 40:15–24), the **serpent in the sea** (Amos 9:3), and
  ultimately the **serpent of Eden** (Gen 3).

## ✝️ Where it all lands: Christ and the dragon
- **Revelation finishes Isaiah's sentence.** "The great, fiery red **dragon**... that **serpent of old**,
  called the Devil and Satan" (Rev 12:3, 9) — with **seven heads** (12:3), the Leviathan picture exactly. He
  is bound (Rev 20:2) and finally destroyed (Rev 20:10). And the new creation has a stunning detail: **"there
  was no more sea"** (Rev 21:1) — the home of chaos is gone for good.
- **The Fathers and the fishhook 🎣.** The Fathers loved Job 41:1, "Can you draw out Leviathan with a
  hook?", *you* can't, but **God did**. St Gregory of Nyssa (Great Catechism, ch. 24) pictures the Cross as
  God's fishing line: the devil swallowed the **bait** of Christ's visible humanity and was caught on the
  **hook** of His hidden divinity. St Gregory the Great's *Moralia on Job* reads all of Job 41 as a portrait
  of satan, "king over all the children of pride" (41:34), pride being the devil's signature sin. Death
  swallowed Christ on Good Friday and was burst open from the inside on Pascha, this is the **Harrowing of
  Hades** King has already studied (lesson of 2026-06-23).
- So the full arc in one breath: **created thing (Psalms) → untameable chaos (Job) → figure of satan
  (Isaiah, Revelation) → hooked, bound and finished in Christ.**

## ⚖️ Honest flags (sacred accuracy)
- Whether Job 41 describes a real animal in poetic dress (crocodile), a chaos-symbol, or both, is a
  legitimate open question; the OSB and the Fathers happily hold layers together (literal + typological).
  There is **no Church dogma** requiring one view.
- Later Jewish writings outside the canon (e.g. 4 Ezra 6:49–52; 1 Enoch 60) grew legends of Leviathan and
  Behemoth as a pair kept for the end of days, background colour, **not Scripture**.
- The Ugarit/Lotan parallel is standard, well-attested scholarship, but the *use* made of it above (Scripture
  demythologizing the pagan monster) is the mainstream believing reading, worth enjoying, held humbly.
- As always: this is a **study aid**. For doctrine and anything pastoral, **Fr Bogdan is the authority**, a
  lovely one to ask him: "How do the Fathers read Leviathan in Job 41?"

## 🕊️ How to live it (for Adèola and King)
1. **Name your Leviathan.** Everyone has a sea monster, the fear, the debt, the diagnosis, the chaos you
   cannot tame. Job 41's question to you is the same as to Job: *can you hook it?* No. **He can.**
2. **Pray Psalm 103 (LXX) in the evening** like the Church does at Vespers, and when you reach the dragon
   made "to play", smile: your God is that much bigger than your problems.
3. **Refuse the devil his PR.** Scripture never presents evil as God's equal opposite. Leviathan is a
   creature, on a leash, already hooked. Fear God, not the dragon.
